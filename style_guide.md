# Style Guide

This style guide dictates the tone, structure, vocabulary, and formatting for all job application responses. The objective is to present as a highly capable, adaptable, and articulate candidate who writes with clarity and authenticity.

## Language and Spelling

- Strictly British English: Always use UK spelling conventions (e.g., realise not realize, analyse not analyze, programme, rigour, behaviour).
- Clear and Simple English: Favour clarity over complexity. Write in plain, accessible English. Avoid overly convoluted sentence structures.
- No Corporate Fluff: Strictly avoid ornate, flowery, or overly corporate jargon (e.g., do not use words like synergy, game-changer, dynamic, or visionary). Keep the language grounded and factual.
- No AI-sounding Words/Phrases: Strictly avoid common words or phrases that sound clearly AI-generated (e.g., hands-on, robust, rigorous, streamline, passion, tangible)

## Tone and Voice

- Authentic and Direct: Sound like a genuine, intelligent graduate student. The tone should be professional, confident, and earnest, but never arrogant or robotic.
- Action-Oriented: Use the active voice. Focus on what I did, how I adapted, and what I learned.
- Evidence-Based: Let the facts and experiences speak for themselves. Instead of claiming to be "highly analytical," describe an instance of solving a complex technical problem.

## Structure and Strategy (Maximising Marks)

- Direct and Comprehensive: Explicitly answer every part of the prompt in the opening sentences. Do not bury the main point.
- Marking-Scheme Aligned: Structure the response to make it incredibly easy for a recruiter to tick off competencies. If a question asks for multiple skills (e.g., technical ability and communication), dedicate a distinct, easily scannable section or paragraph to each.
- STAR Method (Seamlessly Applied): When recounting experiences, naturally follow the Situation, Task, Action, Result framework without making it read like a rigid template. Emphasise the Action (what was done and how) and the Result (the outcome and what was learned).

## Key Thematic Emphases

- Adaptability and Rapid Learning: Frequently highlight the ability to quickly master new frameworks, transition between disciplines (e.g., Chemistry to Computer Science), and proactively self-teach.
- Technical Meets Practical: Bridge the gap between complex technical work (e.g., digital signal processing, LLM prompting) and real-world outcomes or business value.
- Reflective Mindset: Demonstrate an understanding of the broader context of your work, such as the ethical, computational, or business implications of the technologies used.

## Examples

The following paragraphs are purely to demonstrate the tone and voice your writing should take on. The same exact wording and phrasing should not be used in your responses.

Example 1:

> I am applying for the Software Engineer track because of the wide variety of projects and experience it offers. Having transitioned into computing over the past year through my Master's degree, I have built a strong technical foundation and discovered an enthusiasm for the field. I am eager to rapidly expand my practical skills in developing large-scale software systems. Capgemini’s strong emphasis on continuous learning aligns perfectly with my desire to explore diverse technological challenges and discover the specialised areas of software engineering where I can make the most impact.
>
> For my Master's final project, I evaluated the practical range at which contactless card transactions could be intercepted using accessible radio hardware. To achieve this, I engineered custom digital signal processing pipelines in Python using the GNU Radio toolkit. My pipelines took raw digital radio signals, recovered the encoded binary information, and converted it into a hexadecimal output compliant with NFC communication protocols. Because GNU Radio relies heavily on object-oriented programming, I had to quickly master the framework's architecture, structuring my processing chain into cleanly encapsulated classes.
>
> During this project, I also modernised my workflow by actively leveraging LLMs to assist with code generation. By researching and applying advanced prompting techniques, such as chain-of-thought reasoning, persona adoption, and eliciting clarifying questions, I successfully engineered custom state machines to handle complex bit manipulation and noise error correction. I also utilised Git throughout the project for version control.
>
> This experience directly prepared me for the Software Engineer pathway by demonstrating my ability to rapidly learn complex, unfamiliar frameworks. Transitioning from the web development I had done previously to low-level signal processing required significant adaptability. Ultimately, the project taught me how to leverage programming not just to build functional systems, but to creatively solve intricate technical problems, a mindset I am excited to bring to Capgemini.

Example 2:

> The rapid advancement of AI models sparked my curiosity. While my course emphasised mastering fundamental coding skills before using AI, I recognised that modern software engineering requires a strong grasp of these tools.
>
> After a module briefly introduced few-shot prompting and chain-of-thought reasoning, I actively researched effective ways to code using LLMs. During my final project, I put this into practice for writing Python scripts. I experimented with assigning personas, instructing the model to ask clarifying questions, and using meta-prompting strategies, which resulted in much cleaner and more accurate code.
>
> I learned that AI is incredibly powerful when guided systematically, but it requires careful oversight. I am now eager to expand my knowledge by exploring agentic AI, integrating these tools into my development workflow, and better understanding the financial, environmental and computational costs of AI to determine when its use is appropriate.

Example 3:

> I am interested in the Graduate Engineer - Security Research and Testing role at UKTL because of the unique opportunity it presents in gaining practical experience early on in my career in cyber security research, and the structured learning and support that UKTL offers to go along with it. This opportunity really caught my attention because it aligns perfectly with my Master's research project and my future career aspirations. I am also motivated by the idea of my work serving the public good, and security research on national communications infrastructure would be a great cause to be a part of.
>
> In my recently-completed Master's in Computer Science, I undertook a cyber security research project for my dissertation. The scope of this project was to determine the physical distance that NFC communication between a contactless payment card and a reader could be eavesdropped by a third-party antenna. To achieve this, I had to quickly learn complex open-source digital signal processing software (GNU Radio), build custom digital signal processing pipelines, and use communication protocols as defined in the ISO/IEC 14443 standard to convert the raw radio signals into readable hexadecimal messages using Python scripts. As a result, I was able to eavesdrop on and reconstruct the messages sent between the card and reader at a distance of 10 cm, and showed that a covert antenna could receive these messages even when placed underneath a desk - a novel attack vector, and new contribution to this research area. I thoroughly enjoyed this project, and believe that the rapid learning, problem solving and attention to detail that this project required makes me an excellent candidate for the security research and testing graduate programme.
>
> Alongside the research project, I participated in the university's ethical hacking society on a weekly basis, tackling CTF challenges in a group setting. I also attended an extra-curricular lecture series on common tools in the technology industry, such as Linux, Git and cloud computing. I have been using a Linux laptop for my studies, and have become familiar with the command line interface and tools. Finally, I achieved 89% in the taught module on computer systems and networks, providing me with a deep technical understanding of these areas.
>
> Outside of my studies, I run a community social chess club. This has been a great experience to learn more about leadership, communication and conflict resolution. I have needed to communicate with members of the community from a variety of backgrounds, ages and abilities. I have succeeded in creating a welcoming and inclusive environment, growing the average weekly attendance from 5-10 when I took over leadership to 15-20 today. I believe these skills will help me greatly to succeed in a career at UKTL.
